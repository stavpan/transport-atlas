# Panta Rhei

> **πάντα ῥεῖ** — *all things flow*. Attributed to Heraclitus of Ephesus (c. 535–475 BC).
>
> "No man ever steps in the same river twice, for it is not the same river and he is not the same man."
>
> The atlas takes this as a **working hypothesis, not a slogan**. The hypothesis has a provable core
> (a quantity can be counted and its changes accounted for by movement — the
> [balance law](../01-foundations/01-balance-laws-and-flux.md)), a set of mechanisms that ride on it
> ([part 12](../12-recurring-mechanisms/index.md)), and a boundary beyond which it stops being helpful
> ([the limits of the claim](../11-universality/09-limits-of-the-claim.md)). This part is where the
> hypothesis is *enjoyed* as well as tested.

## Why this name

Panta Rhei is what the atlas actually is. Not a catalogue of equations, but the observation that the
world is made of things *passing through*: water in a river, heat in a wall, cars on a road, opinions in a
population, probabilities in a mind, packets in a fibre. The Greek phrase has the right three-word brevity
for a title, and the right three-thousand-year provenance for a theme that keeps being rediscovered —
by fluid dynamicists, by criticality theorists, by network scientists, by anyone who has ever watched a
river and wondered what, exactly, is persisting.

## The epigraph of the site

> **All things flow — so the only honest way to describe anything is to say what moves, what carries it,
> and what is conserved while it does.**
>
> The Transport Atlas writes that sentence in fifteen hundred ways, and Panta Rhei is the shortest of
> them.

If you are here for the mathematics, start at [the atlas](../00-atlas/index.md). If you are here for the
theme, read the six notes above in order; the first three are about *what flows*, the last three about
*what that costs*: direction, memory, and the impossibility of cleanly separating one field from another.
